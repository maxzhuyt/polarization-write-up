# Introduction — Draft 16

The set of political questions a survey can ask is finite; the set of questions on which a society can divide is not. Four decades of opinion research have charted the contours of American partisan division — its ideological and affective dimensions, the role of sorting, and the issues that divide most sharply (1–7). This literature is extensive and coherent, but its dominant instrument imposes a bandwidth constraint that shapes what the field can discover. Nationally representative surveys excel at monitoring trends on fixed items yet cover a menu of topics locked in years before fielding — the recent 3 releases of the GSS, for instance, track roughly 200–300 items out of the effectively unbounded space of political topics. Formal taxonomies now distinguish at least nine senses in which a population can polarize (8), yet our understanding of the landscape is limited not by the sophistication of measurements but by the availability of data: instruments capturing pre-selected topics cannot map the full structure of disagreement. The field has thoroughly characterized a fixed set of measurements while the phenomenon itself — which topics divide, along what dimensions, with what intensity — evolves faster than item batteries can be redesigned. What is missing is a map of the opinion landscape at scale — one that requires shifting from hypothesis confirmation on pre-selected items to the integrative, exploratory mapping that Almaatouq et al. (9) argue is necessary for cumulative social science.

This constraint is especially pressing given what we now know about the scope of partisan identity. Party identification, once understood primarily as a policy evaluation or inherited affiliation (10, 11), is now recognized as a social identity akin to religion or ethnicity — one that shapes how voters evaluate information rather than changing in response to it (12). Mason (3) argues that this identity has become a "mega-identity," absorbing racial, religious, class, and cultural identities into partisan alignment, so that "a single vote can now indicate a person's partisan preference as well as his or her religion, race, ethnicity, gender, neighborhood, and favorite grocery store." If partisan identity now organizes lifestyle, consumption, and cultural choices — not just policy positions — then the domain of potentially partisan topics extends far beyond what any fixed survey battery covers. Gadarian, Goodman, and Pepinsky (13) demonstrated how far this reach extends: in the first week of the COVID-19 pandemic, partisanship predicted health behavior more powerfully than income, education, or local disease severity. The implication is that the set of topics on which partisan divergence potentially exists is vastly larger than the set on which it has been measured.

The tradition of extracting coherent signal from sparse, noisy opinion data has a long methodological history. Stimson's Policy Mood (14) aggregates hundreds of irregularly observed survey series into a single measure of preferences for government activity, exploiting the insight that individual responses are noisy — "samples from a host of competing considerations" in the Zaller-Feldman framework (15, 16) — but that covariation across items reveals a latent common factor explaining substantial variance across many domestic policy series. Caughey and Warshaw (17) extended this logic with a group-level IRT model in which a respondent need answer only a single question to contribute to a latent trait estimate, opening decades of sparse historical data to scaling. Fowler et al. (18) decomposed survey respondents into types — showing that 73% of Americans have views well described by a single ideological dimension — using mixture models within the IRT framework. These approaches share a common logic: pool across noisy individual observations to extract a meaningful aggregate signal about the structure of opinion. In this study, we apply the same aggregation logic to a fundamentally different data source.

Computational text analysis was the first attempt to widen the bandwidth of opinion measurement beyond surveys, estimating partisan language in congressional speech, social media, and news corpora (19–21). Continuous partisanship scores (22) and automated framing analysis moved the field beyond binary classification, but a core limitation persists. Systematic external validation against independent survey benchmarks remains rare across 154 reviewed studies (21), and linguistic divergence need not track attitudinal divergence — Gentzkow, Shapiro, and Taddy (22) showed that their partisan language measure captures rhetorical innovation at least as much as policy disagreement. The bandwidth widened, but the inferential gap between language and belief remained. Recent work using large language models to generate synthetic survey responses (23, 24) offers a complementary approach, yet operates on model's response outputs shaped by decoding strategy and alignment tuning, inheriting the same text-surface constraints.

Here we show that the internal representations of large language models provide a measurement signal for partisan divergence that is validated against surveys but not bounded by them. Prior work treats language models as respondents; we treat them as measurement instruments. Like prior work, we prompt models with politically relevant text; unlike prior work, we analyze the internal activation geometry at the moment a model processes a political prompt — before any token is generated. In fact, we use model's internal representation as a huge feature space unparalleled by any previous human data. 

The method works by prompting a language model to simulate congress politicians' positions on topics, then measuring partisan structure in the model's internal activations — the attention-head outputs — rather than in its generated text. For each topic, we compute the Mahalanobis distance between Democratic and Republican centroids in per-head PCA-reduced activation space; the key outcome is how well this activation-derived partisan divergence correlates with the corresponding partisan gap measured in GSS survey data. We test three prompt framings — asking the model to state a politician's position, generate a statement in their voice, or predict their survey response — and find that the stance condition (direct belief-attribution) is most reliable at scale. Pragmatically, we find that politician simulation — using all 550 members of the 116th Congress as high-information partisan cues — produces substantially stronger alignment with survey partisanship (r up to 0.74) than an alternative approach in which we replace politician names with the demographic profiles of real GSS respondents (r = 0.31–0.51). This asymmetry is itself theoretically informative, as we discuss below.

The theoretical basis for expecting convergence between this geometry and survey-measured opinion follows from models of opinion formation in which mass attitudes are structured by elite discourse. Key (25) argued that "the voice of the people is but an echo"; Zaller (16) formalized this in the RAS model, arguing that mass opinion is the joint product of elite discourse, individual awareness, and predispositions. If survey responses are noisy draws from considerations whose partisan valence is set by elite cues, and if language models — trained on the very corpora through which that discourse circulates — encode the statistical regularities of elite-organized partisan structure, then both instruments should register the same underlying partisan geometry: the model from the production side and the survey from the reception side. We call the quantity this method measures *discursive partisan separation*: the degree to which the full public discourse positions the two parties distinctly on a given topic, as encoded in the model's learned representations.

Discursive partisan separation conflates several components that the survey tradition carefully distinguishes: rhetorical positioning (what politicians say), attributed opinion (what public discourse associates with a political actor), affective valence (the emotional charge of the association), and policy substance (the actual policy content). We cannot separate these; an activation pattern is a compressed representation of everything the model learned about the co-occurrence of a political name and a topic in its training corpus. This conflation is a known limitation — and a potential strength. The measure captures the full discursive and socio-cognitive footprint of partisan association, which may be more comprehensive than any single survey item. That these merged components, taken together, correlate strongly with the survey-measured attitudinal gap indicates they are organized along the same partisan axis — consistent with the finding across the literature that ideological divergence, affective polarization, and identity sorting, while theoretically distinct (4, 5, 26), are empirically correlated. Because a model can process any natural-language prompt, this approach has a property that surveys and text classifiers lack: generative capacity. The space of measurable topics is bounded only by the granularity of language itself, and the scholar's imagination.

We test this by applying the method to all 550 members of the 116th U.S. Congress as reference points across 199 political topics drawn from the General Social Survey, using 17 models spanning seven architectural families (3B–72B parameters). For each topic, we compute the Mahalanobis distance between Democratic and Republican centroids in the activation space of each attention head — a measure that accounts for covariance structure rather than treating dimensions independently — and correlate this distance with the corresponding GSS partisan gap. The best-performing model (Dolphin3.0-Mistral-24B, an uncensored fine-tune) achieves r = 0.74 on 126 public issues; across all models, best-in-family correlations range from r = 0.65 to 0.74 (Spearman rho up to 0.83). Cross-architecture agreement between architecturally distinct models (Yi-34B and Mistral-24B) reaches rho = 0.83, indicating that the signal reflects the partisan structure of discourse rather than idiosyncrasies of any single model. Given that survey-based partisan gaps are themselves measured with error — individual policy items have reliabilities as low as 0.37–0.55 (27) — the observed correlation may approach the reliability ceiling of the benchmark.

The design of the method illuminates a pattern consistent with the literature on partisan identity. Experimental work has shown that partisan identity signals — party labels, not demographics — are the principal organizers of affective attitudes: when policy preferences are stated directly on party-branded issues (removing the identity signal carried by the party label), the partisanship effect drops by 57% (28). Congressional names function as high-information party-identity cues that activate the model's encoding of the full partisan landscape. The gap we observe between politician-simulation (r up to 0.74) and demographic-simulation — in which we replace politician names with demographic profiles of real GSS respondents (r = 0.31–0.51 depending on profile richness and model) — is directly consistent with this finding. Models encode partisan structure more faithfully through parametric knowledge of named political actors than through inference from demographic correlations, even when those correlations are provided explicitly in the prompt.

The method also reveals a measurement-relevant pattern: uncensored model variants — which retain instruction-following capability but remove RLHF safety alignment — achieve the strongest survey correlations. Dolphin3.0-Mistral-24B (r = 0.74) surpasses every safety-aligned model including those with three times the parameter count; its partially re-aligned variant (Venice, same architecture) reaches only r = 0.64, a 0.10-point gap attributable to alignment tuning alone. The pattern replicates at smaller scale: the uncensored Llama-3-8B dolphin fine-tune (r = 0.66) outperforms standard Llama-3.1-8B instruct (r = 0.65). This raises questions about how alignment tuning interacts with representational fidelity — questions loosely analogous to debates over social desirability bias in surveys (29), where normative pressures reshape the expression of attitudes without eliminating the underlying structure. The partisan geometry is a pretraining artifact; alignment attenuates it but does not erase it.

We then exploit the generative capacity that distinguishes this instrument from surveys. We score over 1.3 million natural-language opinion clauses — assembled from Wikipedia, Reddit, congressional bill titles, and academic abstracts — for partisan divergence using the same pipeline. A surprise analysis decomposes each clause's score into a component predicted by its political surface content and a residual, identifying topics that are more polarized than their surface content predicts: latent partisan structure hidden behind non-political language. The top candidates include everyday interpersonal and lifestyle topics — child-rearing practices, dietary preferences, relationship norms — that carry no partisan vocabulary but are nonetheless assigned large Democrat–Republican activation distances. These findings provide computational evidence for the theoretical prediction that partisan identity has absorbed lifestyle and cultural domains through social sorting (3) — the model detects partisan structure in precisely the topics that mega-identity theory predicts should have become partisan. Out-of-sample validation on 21 hand-curated contemporary topics (the tradwife lifestyle, raw milk consumption, fluoride removal from drinking water) confirms that the method detects latent partisan structure that transfers to genuinely novel domains.

A complementary analysis examines which topics are structurally central to the opinion landscape. We compute four independent fundamentalness measures from the GSS response matrix (11,066 respondents, 98 questions appearing in at least two of the three survey years 2021, 2022, 2024) — mutual information, out-of-sample predictive power, network centrality, and Chow-Liu tree proximity to root — and correlate each with the LLM activation score. Topics that the model flags as most partisan tend to be the same topics that are most structurally central in the survey data: the questions whose answers are most predictive of how a respondent will answer everything else. This convergence between model-derived and survey-derived structure further validates the activation-based measure as capturing a meaningful dimension of the opinion landscape.

We do not claim these models hold political beliefs. We claim that the statistical geometry of their activations — shaped by a broad distribution of partisan text — provides a measurement signal unavailable by other means. Because the method requires only that a social distinction produce distributional traces in language, it generalizes beyond partisanship to any latent dimension — gender, religion, class — that structures text production. More broadly, this work demonstrates that AI systems can serve not only as objects of study but as instruments of measurement — mapping territory that fixed item batteries cannot reach, without surveying a single respondent.
